# 哪吒闹海 — Nezha Conquers the Dragon King
## Project Plan & Build Specification

> *A bilingual, illustrated web storybook adapting the classic Chinese folktale **《哪吒闹海》 (Nézhā Nào Hǎi)** for **high-school students** — narrated in Mandarin and English, illustrated in classical Chinese **gongbi heavy-colour (工笔重彩)** painting style, themed around the Shang dynasty world of the Investiture of the Gods.*

## 1. Research Summary

### 1.1 The Story

**《哪吒闹海》** — known in English as **"Nezha Conquers the Dragon King"** — is one of the most celebrated legends in Chinese mythology. Its canonical literary source is **《封神演义》(Fēngshén Yǎnyì / Investiture of the Gods)**, a Ming-dynasty (1567) novel attributed to **Xǔ Zhònglín (许仲琳)**, though the tale predates the novel and is deeply rooted in Daoist and Buddhist folk tradition.

The 1979 Shanghai Animation Film Studio film *Nezha Conquers the Dragon King* — the first Chinese animated film screened at the Cannes Film Festival — is the version most culturally ingrained across generations and will serve as our primary adaptation source.

**The tale, in our adapted version:**

Deep in antiquity, the **East Sea Dragon King Ao Guang (东海龙王敖广)** and his three brother-kings rule the Four Seas. Beneath the waves lies a magnificent **Crystal Palace (水晶宫)** — but the Dragon Kings are tyrants, demanding not only grain and incense from the human world but **child sacrifices (童男童女)**. The people of the coastal fortress **Chentang Pass (陈塘关)** live in terror.

At the mountain fortress of Chentang Pass, the military commander **Li Jing (李靖)** — the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King — has been anxiously awaiting the birth of his third child. His wife, **Lady Yin (殷夫人)**, has carried the child for an astonishing **three years and six months (三年六个月)**. At last, one night, Lady Yin dreams of a Daoist immortal who places something luminous in her bosom — and she gives birth.

But what emerges from the womb is not a baby: it is a **glistening flesh ball (肉球)**. Li Jing, horrified, strikes it with his sword. The ball splits open, and from within leaps a **bright-eyed, radiant boy (可爱的小男孩)** — already able to walk and speak. The child's palms bear the characters **哪 (nǎ)** and **吒 (zhā)** — and so he is named **Nezha**.

The immortal **Taiyi Zhenren (太乙真人)** — master of Kunlun Mountain — descends from heaven, declares Nezha the reincarnation of the Spirit Pearl (灵珠子), and takes him as his disciple. He bestows two divine weapons upon the child: the **Universe Ring (乾坤圈)**, a golden ring of immense power, and the **Red Armillary Sash (混天绫)**, a crimson silk ribbon that commands the tides.

Years pass. Nezha grows into a brave, headstrong, deeply compassionate boy. One sweltering summer day — **he is seven years old** — Nezha asks his mother for permission to play by the sea. At the shore, he bathes in the **Nine-Bend Stream (九湾河)**, letting the Red Armillary Sash trail into the current. The sash sends **tremors through the ocean**, shaking the foundations of the Crystal Palace far below.

**Ao Guang** sends his Yaksha general **Li Gen (夜叉李根)** to investigate. The yaksha confronts Nezha with crude threats — and Nezha, shocked to learn that the Dragon King has been **demanding children as sacrifices**, fights back and kills the yaksha with the Universe Ring.

Ao Guang's **Third Prince Ao Bing (三太子敖丙)** — a proud, cold young dragon warrior — surfaces to avenge the yaksha. He fights Nezha fiercely but is slain. In the original tale, Nezha **pulls out Ao Bing's dragon tendons (龙筋)** and offers them as a belt for his father — a detail that underscores Nezha's innocence; he does not yet understand the weight of what he has done.

When **all Four Dragon Kings** gather before Chentang Pass and threaten to **flood the entire region** unless Li Jing surrenders his son, the weight of Nezha's actions crashes down upon him. In a moment of extraordinary moral clarity — and the emotional core of the tale — Nezha declares:

> *"I am the one who did this. I will not let the people suffer for my actions."*

He turns to his parents, and in the most iconic scene of the legend, **carves his own flesh from his bones and returns his body to his mother and father (析骨还父，析肉还母)** — repaying the debt of his birth. He dies.

But Nezha's story does not end in death. His soul drifts to the mountain of his master. **Taiyi Zhenren**, heartbroken but resolute, gathers **lotus roots (莲藕)** and **lotus leaves (荷叶)** from the Pure Land. He arranges the lotus into the shape of a body, places Nezha's soul within, and breathes life back into him. Nezha rises — reborn from the lotus, now more powerful than before.

Taiyi Zhenren bestows upon the resurrected Nezha two new weapons: the **Fire-Tipped Spear (火尖枪)** and the **Wind-Fire Wheels (风火轮)** — blazing wheels that allow him to fly through the sky. Nezha learns to summon his **Three Heads and Six Arms (三头六臂)** form.

With these new powers, Nezha flies back to the East Sea. In a cataclysmic battle, he defeats all Four Dragon Kings, shatters the Crystal Palace, and **ends the reign of terror over Chentang Pass forever**. The people are saved. Rain comes to the parched fields. Nezha has become something more than a boy — he is a **protector deity (保护神)**, known to this day as **Marshal of the Central Altar (中坛元帅)** and **Third Lotus Prince (莲花三太子)**.

**Moral:** Justice sometimes requires extraordinary courage — the courage to confront the consequences of one's actions, to sacrifice for others, and to rise again with purpose. True strength is not power; it is moral responsibility.

### 1.3 Historical Setting & Theme

The tale is set in the **Shang Dynasty (商朝, c. 1600–1046 BC)** — the era of the Investiture of the Gods — though the canonical text (*Fengshen Yanyi*, 1567) is a **Ming-dynasty** novel. The visual world we construct will therefore layer two historical aesthetics:

- **Shang Dynasty (殷商) motifs** — the story's setting: bronze ritual vessels (鼎, 觚), oracle bone inscriptions, jade ornaments, fortress architecture, the sea as an untamed frontier
- **Ming Dynasty (明代, 1368–1644) artistic tradition** — the painting and illustration style: this is when *Fengshen Yanyi* was written and first illustrated; it is also the period most associated with the mature development of **gongbi (工笔) painting** alongside **heavy-colour mineral pigments (重彩)**

**Thematic colour palette anchor:** the **five sacred colours (五色)** of Chinese tradition — **red (朱砂 / cinnabar), blue-green (石青 / azurite), gold (金 / gold-leaf), white (白 / chalk-white), black (墨 / ink-black)** — filtered through the gongbi heavy-colour aesthetic: saturated, layered, jewel-like mineral tones on silk or sized Xuan paper. This is distinctly different from the restrained ink-only palette of the sibling apps' shuimo style.

### 1.4 Gongbi Heavy-Colour Painting (工笔重彩) — Art Style Reference

This is the key differentiator from the sibling storybook apps, which all use **shuimo ink-wash (水墨写意)**. Our Nezha illustrations use **gongbi heavy-colour (工笔重彩)** — a technique with entirely different visual characteristics:

**Gongbi (工笔 — "meticulous brush") core principles:**
- **Precise, controlled outlines** — every figure, wave, cloud, and flame is drawn with fine, deliberate brushstrokes of uniform weight; there is no expressive variation in line thickness for its own sake (opposite of the "beauty of lines" in shuimo)
- **Layered mineral colour washes** — pigment is applied in thin, translucent layers (三矾九染 — "three alum, nine dyeings"), building up rich, luminous colour that glows from within; this is why heavy-colour gongbi looks jewel-like and almost luminous on silk
- **Narrative/figural subjects** — gongbi historically served court painting and Buddhist scroll illustration; it depicts people, dragons, architecture, and battle scenes with the clarity needed to tell a story
- **Fine decorative detail** — dragon scales, armour patterns, wave foam, flame tongues, lotus stamens are all rendered with individual attention
- **Background treatment** — unlike shuimo's generous blank space (留白), gongbi heavy-colour tends toward **full-scene saturation**: the entire composition is painted, with mineral colour filling the surface; negative space is used sparingly and intentionally

**What this means for our image prompts:**
- Each prompt will call for **"gongbi heavy-colour style, meticulous fine brushwork, layered mineral pigments on silk, rich saturated colour"** — NOT "ink-wash" or "shuimo" or "blank space"
- Scenes will be **densely composed and richly coloured** — the opposite of the airy, misty shuimo style of jade-rabbit and magic-paintbrush
- The visual mood is **dramatic, vivid, narrative** — like looking at a Tang-dynasty Buddhist scroll painting or a Ming-dynasty illustrated novel plate
- Key tones: deep **azurite blue (石青)** for sea and sky, **cinnabar red (朱砂)** for flames and Nezha's sash, **gold (金)** for the Universe Ring and divine light, **jade green (石绿)** for lotus leaves and seafoam, **ink-black outlines (墨线)** binding everything together

**Historical precedents to evoke in prompts:**
- The **Tang-dynasty Guanyin of the Southern Sea** silk scroll (South Sea Guanyin, 敦煌)
- **Zhang Xuan (张萱)** and ** Zhou Fang (周昉)** Tang court lady paintings — the richness and delicacy of gongbi figure painting
- The **Ming-dynasty illustrated editions** of *Fengshen Yanyi* and *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* — narrative gongbi illustration plates
- The **1979 Nezha Conquers the Dragon King** animation itself — which was designed in a gongbi-meets-Tang-painting aesthetic by art director **Zhāng Dīng (张仃)**, and is the single most iconic visual reference for this tale

## 2. Story Adaptation — 8 Chapters for High-School Students

### Audience calibration

The user specified **high-school students**. Compared to the sibling apps (targeted at primary-school children), our text will:
- Use **longer, more complex sentences** — compound and complex structures, subordinate clauses
- Employ **richer vocabulary** — words like 犹豫 (hesitate), 抗拒 (resist), 尊严 (dignity), 救赎 (redemption), 象征 (symbolize)
- Explore **moral ambiguity** — Nezha is not simply "brave boy defeats dragons"; he causes harm, faces consequence, and his suicide is an act of profound moral reckoning, not a cartoon
- Include **cultural context cues** — references to 龙王, 封神演义, 陈塘关, without over-explaining, trusting the reader's background knowledge
- Allow **thematic depth** — the father-son conflict, the meaning of sacrifice, the tension between individual courage and collective safety

## 5. Image Generation (`generate-images.py`)

### 5.1 Technical Setup

- **Model:** `@cf/black-forest-labs/flux-1-schnell` (1024×768, 4 steps, guidance 7.5) via Cloudflare Workers AI — same proven setup as the reference apps
- **Output:** `images/raw/chapter-N.webp` → copied to `images/chapter-N.webp` for serving
- **Idempotent:** skips existing files; retry on timeout/5xx
- **Reference for API/HTTP shape:** `~/Projects/magic-paintbrush/generate-images.py`

### 5.2 Shared Style Prefix (prepended to every prompt)

> **"Chinese gongbi heavy-colour painting (工笔重彩画), meticulous fine brushwork, layered mineral pigments on sized silk, rich saturated jewel-like colour, precise controlled outlines, Shang dynasty aesthetic, Ming dynasty illustration tradition, deep azurite blue, cinnabar red, jade green, gold accents on dark silk ground, dramatic narrative composition, masterpiece quality."**

This prefix ensures every generated image is in the gongbi heavy-colour style — NOT shuimo ink-wash, NOT modern digital art, NOT watercolour. The key terms that anchor the style: **gongbi**, **heavy-colour**, **mineral pigments**, **silk**, **precise outlines**, **saturated colour**, **Shang dynasty**, **Ming illustration**.

### 5.3 Per-Chapter Prompts

Each prompt includes: the shared prefix + scene description + cultural anchoring + mood keyword.

## 8. Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| FLUX renders shuimo/ink-wash instead of gongbi when prompted | Strong, specific prefix: "gongbi heavy-colour, meticulous fine brushwork, layered mineral pigments on silk, saturated jewel-like colour" — every prompt opens with this; regenerate any shuimo-looking duds |
| Dark silk ground makes text hard to read | High contrast: chalk-white (`--chalk-white: #F0EDE4`) text on dark (`--silk-dark: #1C1820`); verify WCAG AA contrast ratio; test on mobile in bright light |
| The self-sacrifice scene (Ch 6) is too intense for the audience | Text: restrained, dignified, emotional rather than graphic — "he held the blade with calm resolve" not "he cut himself"; image: "no gore, only the emotional weight visible in postures"; high-schoolers can handle the moral weight, just not gratuitous gore |
| Nezha's actions (killing Ao Bing, pulling tendons) may seem morally ambiguous to some readers | Lean into it — high-school level *requires* moral complexity; add optional contextual annotation: "In the 1979 film, Nezha's killing of Ao Bing is reframed as justice against a tyrant who devoured children" |
| Gongbi prompts may produce overly busy/detailed images that look cluttered at 1024×768 | Add "masterpiece quality, balanced composition, clear focal point" to each scene prompt; post-process: crop to 1024×768 at the most readable portion |
| Pinyin inaccuracies | Hand-author and proof-read pinyin blocks; cross-check with `pypinyin` library if needed; mark any uncertain tones |
| Dark theme may feel unusual for a "children's story" app | This is intentionally *not* a children's app — it targets high-school students; the dark ground signals maturity and drama; siblings' light backgrounds are for children; Nezha's dark ground is for young adults |
| Father-son conflict (Li Jing vs Nezha) may resonate negatively with some readers | Handle with care: Li Jing's fear is understandable (his family faces annihilation); Nezha's sacrifice is framed as heroic, not as a family tragedy; the resolution (lotus rebirth) is hopeful |
